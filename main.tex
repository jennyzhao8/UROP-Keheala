This supplementary document presents descriptive statistics and data quality assurance (DQA) analyses conducted as part of the Keheala Study 2 randomized controlled trial, which evaluated a digital adherence support platform for tuberculosis (TB) patients in Kenya. The DQA assesses recording errors in TIBU, Kenya's national electronic TB reporting system, by treating a contemporaneous paper registry audit as the ground truth. The TIBU dataset available for this study covers patients registered between January 2017 and December 2021 ($N = 119{,}653$ nationwide). A paper registry audit was conducted across study clinics between November 2018 and September 2019, yielding $3{,}645$ audited records, of which $3{,}370$ could be matched to a corresponding TIBU record and form the basis of the DQA. Tables~\ref{tbl:patient_char} and~\ref{tbl:outcomes} characterize the study population across three nested cohorts: all patients registered in TIBU during the study period, patients at study clinics in TIBU (the MITT sample), and the subset of study-clinic patients whose records were audited in the DQA.

The core of the DQA compares treatment outcomes recorded in TIBU against outcomes recorded in the paper registry for each matched patient. Three types of discrepancy are distinguished. Type I errors occur when TIBU records a successful outcome (treatment completion or cure) but the paper registry records a failure (death, treatment failure, or loss to follow-up), representing a false-positive in the digital record. Type II errors are the reverse: TIBU records a failure while the paper registry records success, representing a false-negative. Incomplete-data discrepancies arise when TIBU contains no outcome but the paper registry does. The cross-tabulation in Table~\ref{tbl:crosstab} maps the full distribution of these discrepancies across outcome categories; subsequent tables stratify error rates by clinic characteristics (province, urbanicity, and size) and patient characteristics (sex, age, disease type, and HIV status); and the time-series figures trace how error rates vary with the age of the patient record and across the study period.
