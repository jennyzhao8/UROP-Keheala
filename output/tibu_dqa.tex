Since its introduction in 2012, Kenya's TIBU database has increasingly served as the source for peer-reviewed tuberculosis research in Kenya, including national analyses of treatment interruption and mortality \citep{masini2016survival, onyango2017reduction}, HIV--TB co-infection outcomes \citep{kosgei2020gender, ngari2023burden}, pediatric TB epidemiology and forecasting \citep{onyango2018pediatric, siamba2023application}, drug-resistant TB  \citep{mutabari2022drtb}, county-level retrospective cohorts of treatment outcomes and mortality \citep{abdullahi2019mortality, kimani2021factors, katana2022kilifi, ngari2025mortality}, community health volunteers' contribution to case notification \citep{abongo2020chv}, and national estimates of TB under-reporting \citep{tollefson2016underreporting}.  Despite the growing reliance on TIBU, its accuracy remains relatively under-studied.  A high quality audit of $N=222$ cases registered in 2012 found that, three years later (in 2015) TIBU was more complete than TB treatment cards and treatment clinic registries.  However, when a patient's paper records could be located, they often did not concord with TIBU, e.g., over 30\% of outcome data in TIBU did not concord with the outcomes in the paper records \citep{sharma2015dataquality}.  In subsequent years, TIBU's recording and reporting tools were revised and a recurring data-quality-assessment (DQA) programme was instituted \citep{ntldp2019dqa, ntldp2022dqa}; the most recent DQA (2022) reports high concordance between TIBU and the facility TB register ($\sim$89\% of treatment outcomes, Cohen's $\kappa \approx 0.86$) \citep{ntldp2022dqa}. These assessments are internal program self-audits on small, purposively selected samples, however, and TIBU has not since been the subject of an independent, systematically sampled audit.  

This study helps to fill that gap.  It presents exploratory, secondary analyses of an audit of $N=\Nselected$ TIBU records, which was conducted as part of a trial evaluating a digital adherence tool called Keheala \citep{yoeli2026digital}.  Although a relatively large number of records was audited, we note up-front that the audit had important limitations: it was restricted to a subset of the Keheala trial participants, and focused exclusively on outcome data; no effort was made to systematically evaluate TIBU beyond this population, or to comprehensively digitize other fields in the registry.  However, despite these limitations, we believe analyses of the audit data can be informative to researchers who rely on TIBU to study tuberculosis in Kenya.


\section{Methods}
\subsection{Data sources and audit sample}
The TIBU extract examined herein covers patients registered between April 2018 and December 2019 ($N = \NallTIBU$ nationwide), the Keheala trial study period.  The data were extracted in December 2021, and treatment outcomes were recorded through late 2021.  The long outcome window reflects standard TB treatment duration: roughly six months for drug-sensitive disease and up to 18--24 months for multidrug-resistant (MDR) disease.

The Keheala trial was restricted to the following counties: Kakamega, Kiambu, Kisumu, Machakos, Mombasa, Nairobi, Turkana, and Wajir.  Additional restrictions included: access to a cell phone, and knowledge of English or Kiswahili \cite{yoeli2026digital}.  Clinic participation in the Keheala trial was voluntary.  Within the study period, $\Npartclinics$ patients were registered in clinics that participated in the trial.

For the audit, Wajir was omitted because it is remote and difficult to reach, and had few participants.  For each of the remaining seven counties, the Keheala trial study team digitized paper-registry records for all Keheala trial participants in two sub-counties.  These sub-counties were selected via the following cluster-randomization procedure.  Within each county, sub-counties were ordered by the number of participants, and a median split was performed, so that one `block' of sub-counties comprised a relatively small group of the largest sub-counties and the other block a larger group of smaller sub-counties.  One sub-county was then drawn from each block to represent the county (two in total: one large, one small), for $\NsubcountiesAudited$ audited sub-counties in all.\footnote{We identify the audited sub-counties operationally as the two sub-counties per county containing the most patients located in the paper audit; this reproduces the two-per-county design and excludes a small number of stray, cross-coded patients in neighbouring sub-counties.}  All $\Nselected$ trial participants registered in these sub-counties during the study period constitute the population from which paper records were sought (the \emph{selected} sample).  

The study team searched clinics' paper registries for these participants in multiple waves, beginning in late 2018 and ending in mid-2020, matching records on SCRN and spot-checking against patient and disease characteristics such as age, sex, and HIV co-infection.  Figure~\ref{fig:consort} summarizes the resulting exclusion cascade.  Of the $\Nselected$ selected participants, $\Nlocated$ were \emph{located} in the paper registry; the remaining $\Nnotlocated$ could not be found and are not auditable.  From the located records we exclude only the $\Nuncheckable$ patients whose paper record contains no classifiable outcome (blank or the interim ``not completed'' code), as the audit requires a ground-truth outcome.  This leaves an \emph{analyzed} sample of $\Nanalyzed$ patients.

The individual and disease characteristics reported in Tables~\ref{tbl:patient_char} and~\ref{tbl:outcomes} are drawn from the TIBU record and defined as follows: a patient is \emph{bacteriologically confirmed} if the baseline (month-0) sputum smear is positive or GeneXpert reports ``MTB detected''; \emph{drug resistant} if a resistance pattern is recorded; \emph{extrapulmonary} if the disease site is coded extrapulmonary; a \emph{retreatment} case if the registration type is relapse or treatment after loss to follow-up; and \emph{HIV positive} if HIV status is recorded positive.

\subsection{Defining and Classifying Discrepancies}
The central outcome of interest is a discrepancy between a patient's record in TIBU and in their clinic's TB register, as digitized by the study team.  A record whose TIBU and paper outcomes agree is \emph{concordant}; a record is a \emph{discrepancy} if the two outcomes disagree, or if the TIBU outcome is missing (blank or the interim ``not completed'' code) while the paper register records one.

TIBU is a paper-to-digital reporting system: clinic staff record treatment data on the paper TB register at the point of care, and TB clerks subsequently transcribe those entries into TIBU.  We therefore treat the paper register as ground truth, so that a discrepancy reflects a gap or error in the transcription into TIBU.  

For many of our analyses, we classify discrepancies into categories as follows.  First, we assign every treatment outcome---in both TIBU and the paper register---to one of three documented states: treatment \emph{success} (cure or treatment completion), treatment \emph{failure} (death, treatment failure, or loss to follow-up), or \emph{transfer-out}. Transfer-out (including the MT4 code, used when drug resistance is discovered mid-treatment and the patient is referred to a drug-resistant TB program) is a documented outcome rather than an exclusion: a transfer recorded in the paper register is ground truth like any other outcome, and a TIBU value that disagrees with it is a countable discrepancy.  

Then, comparing the TIBU-recorded outcome against the paper register yields four categories of discrepancy, each named after the outcome TIBU records when it disagrees with the paper register:

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0pt
    \item \textbf{False positive} (a Type~I error): TIBU records a successful outcome but the paper registry records something else---a failure or a transfer-out. TIBU \emph{overstates} the program's success.
    \item \textbf{False negative} (a Type~II error): TIBU records a failure but the paper registry records something else---a success or a transfer-out. TIBU \emph{understates} the true status.
    \item \textbf{False transfer}: TIBU records the patient as transferred out, but the paper registry records a real success or failure---a patient who was in fact seen through to an outcome at the clinic.
    \item \textbf{Missing in TIBU}: TIBU contains no outcome (blank or the interim ``not completed'' code) but the paper registry records one. This is distinct from loss-to-follow-up, which is itself a recorded outcome: the clinic did record an outcome on paper, but the TIBU entry was never created or finalized.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
Our analyses proceed in three steps. First, the cross-tabulation in Table~\ref{tbl:crosstab} gives the full distribution of outcome discrepancies across the four categories defined above. Second, we break these discrepancies down by \emph{when} they occurred (a smoothed time series over the study period, \S\ref{sec:errors_when}), \emph{where} they occurred (clinic-level, by province, urbanicity, and size, \S\ref{sec:errors_where}), and among \emph{whom} (patient-level, by sex, age, disease type, and HIV status, \S\ref{sec:errors_who}); a multivariate logistic-regression counterpart of the where/who breakdowns, reporting average marginal effects with clinic-clustered standard errors, appears in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_logit}. Third, we use the records' date fields as a sensitivity check on these discrepancy counts (\S\ref{sec:date_errors}).

The time-series smoothing (\S\ref{sec:errors_when}) fits each record's binary discrepancy indicator against its outcome date by locally-weighted regression (LOWESS, span $0.35$), with pointwise $95\%$ confidence bands from a percentile bootstrap; the full estimator is described in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_smoothing}.

A final set of analyses (\S\ref{sec:date_errors}) uses the date fields to probe the assumption that the digitized paper record is ground truth, since the audit's own transcription can err, e.g., through typos, misattribution of a record, or, most relevantly for these analyses, transcription of an out-of-date registry entry. We use three dates per record---the TIBU registration date, the TIBU outcome date, and the paper outcome date---treating implausible values (year before 2010 or after 2025) as missing, and we derive an \emph{outcome date} for the time series (the TIBU outcome date, or the paper outcome date when TIBU is blank, so that missing-in-TIBU records remain plottable) and a \emph{record age} (days from registration to a fixed extract snapshot of 1~January 2022) used as a covariate. We first compare the TIBU and paper outcome dates directly, by discrepancy category (Table~\ref{tbl:date_agree} and Figure~\ref{fig:tibu_vs_paper_date}). We then use these dates to construct a \emph{date-gated} reclassification of discrepancies: a TIBU--paper disagreement is counted as a discrepancy only when the paper outcome date is strictly later than the TIBU outcome date, and is otherwise treated as concordant, on the reading that TIBU carries a post-audit update to the register. Because the audit transcribed dates but did not prioritize their accuracy, these date-field analyses should not be interpreted as conclusive.

\subsection{Preregistration}
No analyses were pre-registered for this study. The Keheala trial's protocol specified the sampling procedure described above, and the trial's own analyses of the audit data focused on overall false positive and false negative error rates, as well as comparisons of these rates across the trial's treatment groups.

\section{Robustness to digitization errors}
\label{sec:correction}
% =============================================================================
\subsection{Case Study}
A researcher who employs a TIBU treatment outcome to develop their study's outcomes can use the discrepancy rates measured here to gauge the impact of digitization errors on their findings.  We now demonstrate such an analysis on the Keheala trial data.  

The audit provides a full \emph{confusion matrix} between the TIBU-recorded outcome and the paper-register ground truth (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}): most records are concordant---successes and failures agree $95\%$ and $91\%$ of the time---but a TIBU transfer-out is a real success or failure $51\%$ of the time, and a record missing from TIBU is a real outcome in $87\%$ of cases.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{fig_confusion_matrix.pdf}
    \caption{Audit confusion matrix: the TIBU-recorded outcome (rows) against the paper-register ground truth (columns), collapsed to success (C/TC), failure (D/F/LTFU), transfer-out, and---for TIBU only---missing/blank. Each cell gives the count and the row share.}
    \label{fig:confusion}
\end{figure}

To correct a reported outcome distribution we reclassify a study's recorded counts through this matrix. Writing $n_S, n_F, n_T$ for the numbers of recorded successes, failures, and transfers, the estimated true counts are $\hat n_c = \sum_{r} n_r\, P(\text{paper}=c \mid \text{TIBU}=r)$ for $c \in \{S, F, T\}$, and the corrected unsuccessful-outcome share is $\hat n_F / (\hat n_S + \hat n_F)$. This single step absorbs false positives, false negatives, and false transfers together. Where a study also reports records missing from TIBU, the missing row of the matrix is applied to them; because such records are usually absent from the analyzed data, this \emph{false-missing} correction is possible for a trial only if it also includes an audit.  
Note that this correction assumes \emph{non-differential} misclassification---error rates that do not differ across the groups being compared. This is consistent with the findings of the Keheala trial \citep{yoeli2026digital}, and reasonable for overall unsuccessful rates or trials that, like the Keheala trial, randomize participants to treatments within clinic.

Among the $14{,}899$ Keheala study-clinic TIBU records with a classifiable success or failure outcome, the reported unsuccessful-outcome share is $10.50\%$. Reclassifying its successes, failures, and $620$ transfer-outs through the matrix raises this to $11.20\%$ at the audit rates ($11.92\%$ at twice the rates); applying the missing row to the $1{,}060$ TIBU-missing records raises it marginally further, to $11.31\%$.


\subsection{The impact of digitization errors on prior research}
Table~\ref{tbl:literature_correction} applies the same correction to the reported unsuccessful-outcome share of four previous TIBU-based studies, identified in a screen of the works cited in the introduction. For Keheala \citep{yoeli2026digital} and \citet{kosgei2020gender} we additionally correct the study's focal across-group difference---the Keheala-versus-control arm difference and the female-versus-male difference, respectively. In every case the overall rate moves by less than a percentage point, and the focal difference \emph{attenuates} slightly toward zero (the expected effect of non-differential misclassification) while remaining statistically significant even at twice the audit's error rates.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================
We find that TIBU concords with the clinics' paper registers on $\Rateconcord\%$ of treatment outcomes.  
Discrepancies are concentrated at a small number of clinics and, for records missing from TIBU, in the early-2020 onset of the COVID-19 pandemic.  Outright flips between successful (C, TC) and unsuccessful outcomes (LTFU, F, D) are rare: conditionally, a TIBU-recorded success is a failure on-paper just $\RateSuccToFail\%$ of the time and a TIBU-recorded failure a success on-paper $\RateFailToSucc\%$.  Our exploratory analyses using date fields suggest that some of these apparent flips are likely audit artifacts: half of the success-to-failure disagreements ($15$ of $30$) carry an earlier paper date than TIBU, consistent with TIBU holding the later, correct outcome.  This lowers the success-to-failure flip rate to $\sim$0.6\%.

Particularly for studies that span multiple treatment sites and focus on treatment success, these findings raise confidence in TIBU as a reliable source of data.  Our overall concordance rate is much higher than that found by the 2015 TIBU audit \citep{sharma2015dataquality} and similar to the rates reported by Kenya's NTLD-P \citep{ntldp2022dqa}.  The findings of several past studies \citep{kosgei2020gender, katana2022kilifi, ngari2023burden, ngari2025mortality} are robust to correction for digitization errors.

Our finding that discrepancies are concentrated both temporally and at the clinic level can, we hope, be helpful in further improving TIBU's concordance with clinics' paper registries, as it suggests it is not day-to-day procedures, but unexpected, temporary clinic-level contingencies which most negatively impact TIBU concordance.  These findings also mean that digitization correction of between-site comparisons benefits from site-specific discrepancy rates.

Finally, our experience highlights the benefits of high-fidelity digitization of fields other than outcomes, such as dates, during an audit.  These fields are valuable of their own right, and can help when, e.g., determining whether the source of a discrepancy is an error in TIBU, or in the audit itself. 

To existing audits, this study adds: independence from NTLD-P, a large sample encompassing many patients across a substantial number of treatment sites with ample temporal variation within clinics, and breakdowns of discrepancies by type.  
The study's primary limitations are those noted at the outset: our sample was selected from the Keheala trial's \citep{yoeli2026digital} participating clinics, which limited its representativeness of all of Kenya's treatment facilities; and it focused on the outcome field alone, which both narrowed the analyses we could undertake and, because other fields---including dates---were not transcribed with the same care, left our date-based analyses exploratory.  TIBU would still benefit from a comprehensive, systematically sampled audit.

\section{Smoothing and confidence bands}
\label{sec:appendix_smoothing}
% =============================================================================

The time series of \S\ref{sec:errors_when} is estimated nonparametrically. For a given discrepancy type, each analyzed record contributes a binary indicator---$1$ if the record is that type of discrepancy and $0$ otherwise---placed on the horizontal axis by its outcome date (the TIBU outcome date where available, or the paper-registry outcome date for missing-in-TIBU records, which have no TIBU date). We smooth this indicator against outcome date by locally-weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS): at each point a tricube-weighted local linear regression is fit to the nearest $35\%$ of records (smoothing span $0.35$, no robustifying iterations), so the fitted value estimates the probability that a record with that outcome date is a discrepancy of the given type---the local discrepancy rate. Pointwise $95\%$ confidence bands are obtained by a percentile bootstrap: the records are resampled with replacement $500$ times, the LOWESS curve is refit on each resample and evaluated on a common grid of $60$ points spanning the 1st--99th percentiles of the outcome-date range, and the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of the bootstrap curves are taken at each grid point; a curve is drawn only where at least $30$ records are available. The same estimator produces the paper-date and date-gated sensitivity versions of the figure (Appendices~\ref{sec:appendix_paper_dates} and~\ref{sec:appendix_gated}), differing only in the date used for the axis or the discrepancy definition.

\section{Multivariate predictors of missing-in-TIBU and false transfer}
\label{sec:appendix_logit}
% =============================================================================

The marginal breakdowns in \S\S\ref{sec:errors_where} and \ref{sec:errors_who} stratify discrepancy rates one characteristic at a time, leaving open whether apparent associations reflect that characteristic or a correlated confound. Table~\ref{tbl:logit_errors} reports logistic regressions of the two discrepancy categories with enough events to identify a multivariate model---missing-in-TIBU and false transfer---on individual, clinic, and record-age covariates jointly. (We model these two rather than false positives or false negatives because false negatives are rare ($\Nfn$ events) and false positives---though numerous ($\Nfp$)---are dominated by audit-snapshot artifacts that the date-gated analysis strips out (\S\ref{sec:date_errors}); their marginal evidence is in Tables~\ref{tbl:clinic_errors}--\ref{tbl:patient_errors}.) Missing-in-TIBU is modelled with province fixed effects; the sparser false-transfer outcome is not, as too few false transfers occur per province to identify province effects (the cluster-robust covariance degenerates). For each outcome, the first column uses the full classifiable sample and the second excludes that outcome's own two dominant clinics (Table~\ref{tbl:culprit_clinics})---clinics 0 and 115 for missing-in-TIBU, clinics 162 and 50 for false transfer---as a sensitivity check, since events concentrated in a couple of clinics could otherwise drive coefficients on covariates merely correlated with their idiosyncratic profile.

For \textbf{missing-in-TIBU}, removing the two COVID clinics collapses both nominally interesting non-temporal coefficients. The point estimate on \emph{Male} moves from $+2.8$~pp to $-0.2$~pp, and the estimate on \emph{Clinic size} from $-1.0$~pp/log-patient to $+0.2$~pp; neither is statistically significant in either specification. \emph{Record age} survives in both columns but its magnitude more than halves (from $-8.4$ to $-3.4$~pp/year, with significance falling from $p < 0.001$ to $p < 0.05$). We read this as a mostly mechanical reporting-lag effect: at any fixed snapshot date, more recent records have had less time to be transcribed into TIBU. The two COVID clinics inflate the slope because their missing-in-TIBU records cluster in the late, COVID-affected portion of the registration window. Outside those two clinics, neither patient demographics nor clinic-level structural features predict TIBU missingness; the only systematic signal is a modest record-age gradient consistent with reporting lag.

\textbf{False transfer} tells the same story as missingness, one clinic-set over. In the full sample three covariates reach significance---\emph{Male} ($+2.2$~pp), \emph{Clinic size} ($+1.4$~pp per log-patient), and \emph{Record age} ($-5.4$~pp/year)---but all three collapse to non-significance once the two Nairobi clinics that drive false transfers (162 and 50) are excluded: Male falls to $+1.9$~pp, Clinic size flips to $-1.1$~pp, and Record age to $-3.2$~pp, none significant, while the event count falls from $130$ to $44$. (At $44$ events that column is underpowered and should be read only for the disappearance of the full-sample signals, not for new ones---the lone significant coefficient there, on HIV status, is a small-sample artifact.) The apparent male and high-volume-clinic tendencies are therefore not a general labelling practice but the profile of those two clinics---just as the demographic gradients in missingness were the profile of clinics 0 and 115. Both discrepancy types are thus localized two-clinic phenomena rather than TIBU-wide patterns, each with its own pair of clinics.
