\section*{Overview}

This supplementary document presents descriptive statistics and data quality assurance (DQA) analyses conducted as part of the Keheala Study 2 randomized controlled trial, which evaluated a digital adherence support platform for tuberculosis (TB) patients in Kenya. The DQA assesses recording errors in TIBU, Kenya's national electronic TB reporting system, by treating a contemporaneous paper registry audit as the ground truth. The TIBU dataset available for this study covers patients registered between April 2018 and December 2019 ($N = 119{,}653$ nationwide). A paper registry audit was conducted across study clinics between November 2018 and September 2019, yielding $3{,}645$ audited records, of which $3{,}370$ could be matched to a corresponding TIBU record and form the basis of the DQA. Tables~\ref{tbl:patient_char} and~\ref{tbl:outcomes} characterize the study population across three nested cohorts: all patients registered in TIBU during the study period, patients at study clinics in TIBU (the MITT sample), and the subset of study-clinic patients whose records were audited in the DQA.

The core of the DQA compares treatment outcomes recorded in TIBU against outcomes recorded in the paper registry for each matched patient. Patients recorded as transferred out in TIBU---including those assigned the MT4 code, used when drug resistance is discovered mid-treatment and the patient is referred to a drug-resistant TB program---are excluded from error classification, as a transfer is not an auditable final outcome. Records where the paper registry has no outcome are also excluded, as the DQA requires ground truth. Among the remaining patients, three types of discrepancy are distinguished. Type I errors occur when TIBU records a successful outcome (treatment completion or cure) but the paper registry records a failure (death, treatment failure, or loss to follow-up), representing a false positive in the digital record. Type II errors are the reverse: TIBU records a failure while the paper registry records success, representing a false negative. False-missing discrepancies arise when TIBU contains no outcome but the paper registry does, representing a gap in the digital record. The cross-tabulation in Table~\ref{tbl:crosstab} maps the full distribution of these discrepancies across outcome categories; subsequent tables stratify error rates by clinic characteristics (province, urbanicity, and size) and patient characteristics (sex, age, disease type, and HIV status); and the smoothed time-series figures trace how error rates vary with the age of the patient record and across the study period.

\subsection{Error Rates Over Time}

\setcounter{figure}{1}
The TIBU dataset used for these analyses reflects a single final extract, with the latest treatment-outcome dates clustering in late December 2021; we treat the snapshot as having occurred on 1 January 2022. Figure~\ref{fig:over_time} plots DQA error rates against outcome date, estimated via locally-weighted regression (lowess); shaded bands show percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (500 resamples). The outcome date is TIBU's when available and the paper registry's otherwise (used for false-missing records, where TIBU is blank).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig_error_over_time.pdf}
    \caption{DQA error rates over time by outcome date (TIBU when available, paper otherwise). Lowess smooth with percentile bootstrap 95\% CI bands.}
    \label{fig:over_time}
\end{figure}

\noindent The dominant feature of the figure is a pronounced peak in the false-missing rate (to roughly 15\%) for records with outcome dates in 2020 Q1--Q2, coinciding with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic response in Kenya. Of the 116 false-missing records in the classifiable sample, 63 fall in these two quarters, and 50 are concentrated at just two clinics whose overall false-missing rates (12.7\% and 15.4\%) are an order of magnitude above the study-wide rate of 3.8\%. At these clinics, paper-registry entries continued through the pandemic but TIBU outcomes were largely not recorded. Treating the TIBU \texttt{NC} (``not completed'') code as a placeholder rather than a medical outcome rolls the affected records into the false-missing category; this accounts for 76 of the 116 (the remaining 40 have TIBU entirely blank). Type~I and Type~II error rates are both low and roughly flat across the study period.

\subsection{Error Rates by Age of Record}

Figures~\ref{fig:age_reg}--\ref{fig:density} re-express the same data against record-level age axes. Figure~\ref{fig:age_reg} plots error rates against record age at snapshot (days from registration to 1 January 2022); Figure~\ref{fig:age_out} plots them against reporting lag (days from outcome date to snapshot, with the axis inverted so that calendar time reads left-to-right like Figure~\ref{fig:over_time}); Figure~\ref{fig:density} compares the empirical distributions of reporting lag across error categories. The same COVID-era false-missing peak is visible as a left-edge spike in Figure~\ref{fig:age_reg} (records registered in late 2019, whose treatments completed in 2020) and as a mid-2020 spike in Figure~\ref{fig:age_out}; the ECDF in Figure~\ref{fig:density} shows the false-missing distribution shifted noticeably toward shorter reporting lags, which is the same COVID-era concentration viewed as a cumulative distribution.
